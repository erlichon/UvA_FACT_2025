\section{Cross-Dataset Structural Robustness}
\label{sec:cross_dataset_robustness}

A core promise of weight-based interpretability is that models learn meaningful geometric features rather than memorizing dataset-specific pixel correlations.
We test whether bilinear MLPs learn \textbf{Platonic forms}---e.g., ``0-ness'' as circularity---rather than idiosyncratic MNIST stroke patterns.

Throughout this section, \textbf{baseline} refers to models without regularization, while \textbf{regularized} refers to full regularization (noise std = 0.5, weight decay = 1.0) as in Table~\ref{tab:reg_configs}.
All models use \textbf{Center-of-Mass (CoM) normalization}: images are translated so intensity-weighted centroids align, forcing the bilinear layer to focus on shape geometry rather than absolute position.
We probe robustness under three distributional shifts:
(1) \textbf{Writer independence}: MNIST $\leftrightarrow$ EMNIST-Digits (same labels, different writers);
(2) \textbf{Domain transfer}: MNIST $\rightarrow$ USPS (different resolution/collection);
(3) \textbf{Geometric semantics}: MNIST $\rightarrow$ EMNIST-Letters, where letters are semantically different but geometrically similar (O$\to$0, I$\to$1, Z$\to$2, S$\to$5).

\subsection{Functional Analysis: Regularization Enables Transfer}

With the regularized CoM model, cross-dataset accuracy is near-perfect and symmetric for MNIST $\leftrightarrow$ EMNIST-Digits (97.5\% and 97.9\%, Table~\ref{tab:mnist_emnist_digits}, Appendix~\ref{app:cross_dataset_results}), confirming that regularized bilinear features do not overfit to MNIST writers.
For domain transfer to USPS (upscaled from $16 \times 16$ to $28 \times 28$), regularization yields +19 pp improvement (72.3\% vs.\ 53.4\%, Table~\ref{tab:usps}, Appendix~\ref{app:cross_dataset_results}).
On geometrically similar letters (Table~\ref{tab:letter_digit}, Appendix~\ref{app:cross_dataset_results}), the model's most confident prediction is exactly the corresponding digit: O$\to$0 (99.6\%), I$\to$1 (87.1\%), Z$\to$2 (88.8\%), S$\to$5 (87.4\%).

\subsection{Structural Analysis: Quadratic Form Similarity}

\subsubsection{The Failure of Cosine Similarity}

Direct eigenvector comparison via cosine similarity fails to distinguish geometrically similar pairs: MNIST-0 vs EMNIST-O (similar) yields 0.358, while MNIST-0 vs EMNIST-X (dissimilar) yields 0.339---virtually identical.
Figure~\ref{fig:cosine_heatmap} (Appendix~\ref{app:cosine_failure}) shows this failure across all digit-letter pairs, while Table~\ref{tab:eigenvector_comparison_0_O_X} provides a detailed per-eigenvector breakdown confirming that mean similarities are nearly identical (0.358 vs.\ 0.339).
The root cause: bilinear eigensystems share eigenvector directions while class information resides in eigenvalue weights (Figure~\ref{fig:3way_comparison}).

\subsubsection{Quadratic Form Similarity}

We propose comparing full decision surfaces via Quadratic Form Similarity:
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{sim}(A,B) = \frac{\operatorname{tr}(A \cdot B)}{\|A\|_F \|B\|_F} \in [-1, 1]
\end{equation}
This incorporates both eigenvector alignment and eigenvalue magnitudes, enabling discrimination between similar (0.400) and dissimilar (-0.060) pairs ($p_{value} < 10^{-4}$).
Figure~\ref{fig:similarity_vs_k} (Appendix~\ref{app:cross_dataset_results}) demonstrates this: similar pairs converge to high similarity around $k=20$ eigenvectors, while dissimilar pairs remain near zero with non-overlapping 90\% confidence intervals.
Figure~\ref{fig:quadratic_heatmaps} (Appendix~\ref{app:cross_dataset_results}) shows clear discrimination: strong diagonal for MNIST vs.\ EMNIST-Digits and bright entries at shape-matched pairs (0--O, 1--I, 2--Z, 5--S).
Table~\ref{tab:ranking_analysis} (Appendix~\ref{app:cross_dataset_results}) confirms specificity: 3/4 pairs rank \#1, with mean rank 1.2 vs.\ random baseline of 13.

\subsection{Conclusion}

Regularized bilinear MLPs learn \textbf{transferable geometric circuits}: the ``0'' circuit is mathematically near-identical to the ``O'' circuit across independently trained models.
This strengthens the transparency claim---eigenvector features represent genuinely reusable shapes, not dataset-specific artifacts.
